# The Raucous Saints of the Rolling Longhouse
## A modular Eldyrwyld faction dossier

### Design Thesis

The Raucous Saints should not feel like a random comedy encounter imported from somewhere else. They should feel like something Elderwyld itself would eventually grow: a moving pocket-culture born from rebellion, roads, brewing, mixed peoples, bad weather, dangerous distances, and the need to keep memory alive without turning it into doctrine.

Mirathorn was founded by people who fled an authoritarian peace and built a new city through cooperation among many peoples. Its culture already carries brewing, spectacle, civic ritual, improvisation, and a frontier willingness to invent new traditions. At the same time, the current threat in the setting has already shown that music, public feeling, and ritual chorus can be used as tools of corruption and control. Dustwalker, the Shepherd’s hymn-work, and the broader cult logic have made song into a battlefield.

The Raucous Saints belong naturally in that thematic space. They are not merely “a band.” They are a counter-hymn. Where the Shepherd narrows humanity into obedience, they widen it into fellowship. Where cult music seduces, isolates, and enthralls, Saint music binds strangers together, reattaches people to their bodies, and gives fear somewhere to go besides inward. They are loud, undignified, holy in a way institutions find embarrassing, and deeply difficult to absorb into any stable hierarchy.

That makes them useful as a module. They do not need to belong to a single city, plot, or patron. They travel. They arrive. They alter tone and stakes. They can rescue, complicate, celebrate, offend, inspire, recruit, disrupt, or drag buried truths into public view. They work because they are mobile, because they are communal, and because they are not trying to become respectable.

### At a Glance

**Primary Name:** The Raucous Saints of the Open Road  
**Common Names:** the Caskwheel Saints, the Ragged Choir, the Rolling Longhouse, the Barrel Hymn, the Riot on Wheels  
**Faction Type:** itinerant fellowship, war-band, performance caravan, mutual-aid cult without the sinister part  
**Core Function in Play:** moving sanctuary, loud ally, social accelerant, anti-cult mirror, modular troublemaker  
**Primary Aesthetic:** Celtic punk, rebel liturgy, road grime, holy graffiti, patched tartans, dented brass, bagpipes, scars, ale foam, stormlight  
**Core Principle:** No tyrant gets the last verse.

### What They Actually Are

The Raucous Saints are a roaming company of musicians, fighters, brewers, riggers, pilgrims, runaways, romantics, failed clergy, ex-soldiers, smiths, and hangers-on who travel the roads of Eldyrwyld in an enormous rolling longhouse that serves at once as tavern, stage, shrine, bunkhouse, barricade, and siege engine.

They are not an army in the formal sense, though they can fight.

They are not a church in the formal sense, though many common folk treat them like saints.

They are not a mercenary company in the formal sense, though they often solve problems for food, casks, fuel, road rights, spare iron, strange ingredients, song-fragments, or the cancellation of unjust debts.

They are a chosen people in the smallest and most dangerous sense: a people who keep choosing one another.

Anyone can belong if they have the heart for it. Humans, dwarves, gnomes, tieflings, goblins, dragonborn, bugbears, halflings, half-elves, washed-up aristocrats, ex-cult singers, tattooed pilgrims, failed students, one-eyed brewers, and anyone else stubborn enough to sing back at the dark. Bloodline is irrelevant. Temperament matters. You do not need talent. You need nerve, endurance, and the willingness to carry somebody else when the road turns ugly.

### Why They Exist in Elderwyld

The Saints make sense as a second-generation or third-generation frontier phenomenon.

After the first great escape from Lundayell and the founding of Mirathorn, Elderwyld would not have stabilized all at once. The distances are long. The roads are dangerous. Towns become isolated. Customs diverge. Old loyalties calcify. New grudges form. In that kind of world, caravans do more than move grain or iron; they move morale, gossip, memory, grief, songs, and proof that the wider world still exists.

The Saints began as one such procession.

In one version of their origin, they were descendants of the escape-fleet: brewers who salvaged cracked casks from airship holds, gnomish mechanics who knew how to coax motion out of broken things, dwarf smith-drummers who kept work rhythm for crews, and hill singers who refused to let the old rebel songs die. In another version, they formed after a plague year when three separate villages would have starved or turned on each other if a caravan had not arrived with beer, music, carpentry, and enough audacity to force a shared feast. In a third version, they began as pallbearers who got sick of burying the dead in silence. All three stories may be true.

What matters is this: they are not outside Elderwyld’s identity. They are one of its natural consequences. A city-state built by escapees, in a continent still becoming real, will produce wandering fellowships who carry freedom not as law but as practice.

### Their Place in the Moral and Thematic Landscape

The Saints are a living rebuke to two equal and opposite failures.

The first failure is empire: peace through control, order without breath, memory turned into civic performance.

The second failure is dissolution: fear, grievance, and corruption so total that people surrender themselves to a chorus that promises purity, hunger, destiny, or belonging purchased at the cost of everyone else.

The Saints reject both. They believe memory must be lived or it rots. They believe joy is a discipline. They believe fellowship should be rowdy enough to expose hypocrisy. They believe ritual without laughter becomes cruelty faster than anybody admits. They believe a crowd can save lives if it learns the right song before somebody worse teaches it the wrong one.

In this sense they are the holy opposite of the Shepherd’s Flock. Both understand rhythm. Both understand the vulnerability of crowds. Both know that song can move bodies before thought catches up. But the Shepherd’s logic is narrowing, compulsory, and consumptive. The Saints are expansive, mutual, and defiant. Their music does not remove the self. It returns people to themselves at a volume high enough to survive panic.

### The Creed of the Open Road

The Saints do not recite commandments. They recite road-creed, usually shouted, half drunk, before departure or battle.

**No tyrant gets the last verse.**  
**No road belongs to fear.**  
**No feast without strangers.**  
**No hymn that cannot be shouted back at the singer.**  
**No comrade buried nameless.**  
**No child surrendered to the dark while one Saint still stands.**

Members disagree about doctrine all the time. They brawl, flirt, steal each other’s boots, argue about rhythm, and carry grudges for years. But on the road these principles hold.

### Their Relationship to the Nameless Goddess

They are not orthodox.

That matters.

The Temple remembers sacrifice with murals, reflection pools, and solemn continuity. The Saints remember sacrifice by refusing to let the living become obedient out of gratitude for the dead. To them, reverence is not quiet. Reverence is feeding people, rescuing fools, hauling the injured, repairing wagons, and turning public despair into enough noise that it cannot congeal into surrender.

Many Saints wear a broken shackle, a split halo, or a bent wheel as devotional symbols. Some call the Nameless Goddess “She-Who-Made-Room.” Others insist she has no pronouns and would hate being turned into a mascot. One subtradition claims the surest prayer is a chorus sung while lifting something too heavy for one body.

A few priests despise them. Many lay worshippers adore them. Some shrines quietly leave out fresh water and beer kegs whenever the Longhouse is expected on a nearby road.

### Sound as Magic, Sound as Weapon, Sound as Refuge

The Saints should be weird about music.

Not polished. Not courtly. Not clean.

Their performances are part concert, part rally, part liturgy, part emergency medicine. They use pipes, fiddles, hammered strings, drums, nails struck against shield rims, stomp boards, breath-chants, keg percussion, cracked bells, throat-song, and crowd call-and-response. They believe every settlement has its own hidden rhythm and that a good band listens before it plays.

Their music can do several things in-world:

**Counter-Hymn Work.** Certain Saints know how to break the emotional architecture of ritual charm. They do not dispel with purity. They overwhelm with competing pattern: laughter, contradiction, memory, vulgarity, and enough communal participation to break passive listening.

**Road-Binding.** Some marches create a sense of reality pressing back against the undefined edges of the world. Travelers describe feeling “more solid” when walking near the wagon while the axle drums are going.

**Fear Venting.** The Saints are experts at giving terror an outlet. Stomp, shout, clap, answer, sing, strike, repeat. The point is not calm. The point is keeping panic social instead of private.

**Witness Work.** They memorize names of the dead and fold them into refrains. People who have lost kin will sometimes walk miles just to hear whether a name has entered the wagon’s songs.

### The Rolling Longhouse

The Longhouse is not a wagon in the normal sense. It is a moving district.

Seen from a distance, it looks as though a timber hall tore itself free from a village, armed itself, set its jaw, and decided to take the road personally.

Its body is built from interlocked blackened beams, salvaged airship ribs, brewery cask-wood, old gate timbers, and lengths of riveted iron etched with knotwork, curses, jokes, memorial names, and repair marks. The roofline is asymmetrical and aggressive: part longhall, part siege roof, part tavern awning, part shrine canopy. Its sides are layered with drop-panels that can fold out into a beer garden, stage, barricade wall, or sleeping platform depending on the hour and the emergency.

Its wheels are the first thing anyone remembers. They are absurdly huge, the height of small windmills, each one slightly different in origin and construction. One is old dwarven oak banded in iron. One is spoked like a city siege wheel. One appears to have living root threaded through its core and wrapped in lacquer and chain. One bears faded carnival paint beneath mud, soot, and protective runes. None of them should move cleanly. Together they thunder.

The prow is capped with a vast boar skull carved from layered timber and bone plate, its tusks shod in metal, its eye sockets lit from within by green-gold lantern fire. When the Saints enter battle-performance mode, the skull exhales sparks, smoke, and alchemical firebursts from hidden vents. Children love it. Magistrates hate it. Cultists rarely enjoy the experience.

The wagon has several distinct zones.

**The War Porch.**  
A forward fighting deck with railings carved in knot patterns and patched with shield-metal. This is where piper and singer stand when the Longhouse comes in hot.

**The Saint’s Stage.**  
A fold-down side platform large enough for six to ten performers, sermonists, dancers, or a full brawl disguised as choreography.

**The Keg Battery.**  
A reinforced side cradle holding shock-strapped casks. Some contain beer. Some contain alchemical foam. Some are blank and can be filled with blessed water, lamp oil, medicinal broth, soup, or emergency sand. Newcomers are strongly discouraged from guessing which is which.

**The Axle Chapel.**  
Beneath the main body sits the thunder-axle: a bizarre fusion of dwarven mechanical craft, gnomish improvisation, storm-rune geometry, and accumulated roadside miracles. The Saints claim the Longhouse likes music and stores impact, chant, and percussion in its motion. The more the wagon is truly played, the harder it is to stop. This might be theology, engineering, or both.

**The Roofwalk.**  
A fighting line, lookout, and pipe-rigging platform. In bad weather it becomes a dangerous holy place. Saints climb it anyway.

**The Common Belly.**  
Interior benches, hammocks, cask racks, instrument hooks, a central long table, a shrine alcove, and enough hidden storage to make customs officers lose the will to live.

The wagon should feel impossible, beloved, and over-repaired. It is not elegant. It is alive with use.

### Other Weird Features of the Longhouse

The Saints continually modify the wagon. Some additions are practical. Some are devotional. Some are inexplicable even to them.

- Bells made from melted-down shackles that ring differently in the presence of charm magic.
- A “sober lantern” that burns blue near lies and red near bad ale.
- Floorboards branded with rescue tallies and names of places where the wagon should have died but did not.
- A hidden compartment known only as the Last Cask, opened only when a Saint is dying or a whole settlement needs hope it has not earned.
- A roof-rig for launching firework saints, weather pennants, signal rockets, and occasional gnomes.
- A pressure organ built from brewery piping and bone reeds, used to make one awful note that can stop a horse, wake the dead, or clear a square.
- Nails hammered in by every full member on joining. The elders insist the wagon remembers who drove each one.

### How the Longhouse Moves

Most people assume it must require monsters to pull it. Sometimes it does. On easy roads the Saints yoke it to whatever team makes logistical sense: draft oxen, great hill ponies, shaggy marsh beasts, or rented teams from offended locals.

But in its proper mythic mode the Longhouse runs on performance.

Not metaphorically. Actually.

When the drums lock with the axle and the singers catch the right cadence, the great body of the wagon shudders, the runes along its braces pulse, and the wheels begin to turn with far more force than the visible draft could explain. This means that when the Saints are in full cry, they can do what a sane wagon cannot: accelerate into a barricade, crest ground too uneven for its size, or appear to arrive exactly when the road most needs them.

Nobody entirely agrees how this works. The gnome riggers say harmonic transfer. The dwarves say stored impact. The clergy say devotion. Rowan says “loud faith and better carpentry.”

### Membership

The Saints do not recruit by lineage, class, or ideology. They recruit by recognition.

A person becomes a Saint when the band looks at them in the middle of pressure and says, in one way or another, there you are.

Maybe the candidate held a line when others broke. Maybe they fed strangers before themselves. Maybe they sang with a broken jaw. Maybe they started as baggage haulers and simply never left. Maybe they were a former enemy who heard the wrong song at the wrong time and could not return to who they were.

Initiation is simple. The candidate must:
1. Add one nail to the wagon.
2. Add one verse to a living song.
3. Share one true grief and one true joke.
4. Eat at the common table.
5. Be vouched for by three Saints from different origins.

After that, they are in. No blood rite. No purity trial. No symbolic death. The road provides enough of that on its own.

### Internal Culture

The Saints run on chosen-family intensity and professional disorder.

They are loud by default, but not shallow. They take jokes seriously and ceremony irreverently. They have fierce standards about certain things and absolute chaos about others.

**Things they take very seriously:**
- carrying the injured
- feeding guests
- keeping instruments battle-ready
- honoring the dead by name
- not leaving children to cruel authorities
- patching the wagon correctly
- never performing a rescue as if it were charity

**Things they do not take seriously enough:**
- permits
- respectable dress
- military posture
- local curfews
- volume limits
- tavern furniture
- whether a sermon contains obscenity

Disputes are settled through a mix of shouting, private talks, brewing contests, song duels, wrestling, and an infamous practice called the Long Carry: if two Saints cannot stop snarling, they are tied to the same supply sled for a full day’s march until they either reconcile or become too tired to maintain the feud.

### Leadership

The Saints do not function as a pure democracy, but neither are they a simple hierarchy. Leadership is contextual.

**On the road:** the Road-Captain calls pace and route.  
**In battle:** the Stage-Marshal calls entries, exits, and shield placement.  
**In camp:** the Cask-Mother or Cask-Father decides table order, ration fairness, and who needs sleep more than argument.  
**In sacred matters:** the Bell-Keeper or Counterhymnist decides what song can be risked.

This structure makes them feel less like a band with backup musicians and more like a moving society.

### Core Members

#### Rowan Vale, called Rattle-Sermon
Human, though weathered enough to read as half relic.

Rowan is the best public face for the Saints if you want one: a scarred, charismatic paladin-bard whose sermons sound like pub fights that somehow became theology. He was likely raised near orthodoxy, broke with it over some combination of authority, grief, and temper, and now carries a cracked bronze handbell from a shrine he either saved or stole from.

He is not gentle, but he is warm. He laughs like somebody kicking in a door. He cannot resist a doomed cause, a dramatic entrance, or the chance to tell a pompous official exactly what song he should go drown himself in.

He believes freedom is sacred because it is the condition under which actual love can exist. He also believes the Saints would become unbearable if anyone let them call themselves righteous for too long.

#### Brigid Nine-Kegs
Dwarf, brewer, bagpiper, and practical saint of respiratory violence.

Brigid is the wagon’s brew-mother, quartermaster, and one of the few people allowed to interrupt Rowan mid-sermon without consequences. Her pipes are wrapped in old tartan, leather, and silver wire, and her sustained notes can rattle shutters, crack weak glamour, or terrify lesser beasts into reconsidering their life choices.

She treats feeding people as both domestic labor and war doctrine. Her ale can taste like oak smoke, honey, iron rain, or the last good memory a person forgot they still had. She has no patience for purity talk, weak stew, or anybody who thinks bagpipes are not a frontline instrument.

#### Tamsin Coilhorn
Tiefling fiddler, lyric-cutter, rumor-priest, and smiling menace.

Tamsin writes verses faster than other people form regrets. She specializes in counter-hymns, satire, stolen refrains, and the art of finding the one line in a local song that can split a crowd toward courage instead of panic. She is beautiful in the knife-edged way that makes nervous people behave strangely around her.

Tamsin’s horns are capped in brass charms, her boots are always half-laced, and her smile means either affection or disaster. She takes great joy in writing songs so catchy that oppressive authorities hear them in markets for months afterward.

#### Marda Fenstep
Bugbear drummer, shield-hauler, dance-caller, and battlefield pulse.

Marda is huge, shaggy, improbably graceful, and the most physically grounding presence on the wagon. Her kit is part drum-circle, part marching cadence battery, part riot-control apparatus. When panic sets in, Marda is the one who reattaches bodies to tempo. Stomp. Strike. Breathe. Again.

She rarely sermonizes. She counts. She watches exits. She notices who has gone too quiet. If the Saints are a family, Marda is the one who notices who has not eaten and who is lying about being fine.

#### Nib and Knack Sootspin
Gnome twins, axle-riggers, pyrotechnic heretics, and licensed terrors.

No one is sure which twin is older and both consider the question a moral insult. They maintain the thunder-axle, lantern systems, stage braces, pressure organs, hidden rails, spark-throwers, and whatever unspeakable mechanism allows the Longhouse to survive a hard turn at full song.

Nib is the better engineer. Knack is the better liar. Together they are responsible for half the wagon’s miracles and all of its insurance problems.

Their pockets contain nails, wire, sugar, fuse cord, copper charms, cheese, and at least one deeply inadvisable prototype at all times.

#### Brother Ash
Human defector, former choirmaster of something he refuses to name.

Brother Ash is older than the rest want him to admit. He sings baritone, keeps odd hours, carves ward-marks into table undersides, and knows far too much about how bad songs get into institutions. He does not lead from the front. He listens from the edge and tells people when a melody has teeth in it.

He is the clearest living bridge between the Saints and the darker musical magic loose in the setting. He is not a victim exactly. He is not absolved either. He is useful, haunted, and trusted because he never asks to be forgiven before work is done.

#### Sable-of-Nine-Nails
Hobgoblin dancer, marshal, and disciplinarian of glorious trash.

Sable keeps the wagon moving on time. She inventories food, repairs harness, assigns watches, breaks up pointless arguments, and can turn any open ground into a drill floor in seconds. Her dance style is all boot percussion, heel sparks, and hard pivots; in battle it becomes crowd control and shield-line footwork.

She is proof that “punk chaos” does not mean lack of discipline. The Saints survive because someone like Sable exists to translate ecstasy into structure.

### Membership Beyond the Core

The Saints should always feel larger than any fixed roster. There are always temporary members, passengers, pilgrims, apprentices, lovers, cousins, old veterans, stray children under protective watch, retired road dogs, freelance cooks, regional musicians swapping verses for a few Reaches, and people who rode with the wagon for a season and still count as family forever after.

This helps the faction stay modular. You can scale them up or down depending on tone. They can be eight people and a miracle or forty people and a moving festival-state.

### Visual Identity

The Saints do not wear uniforms. They wear allegiance assembled from scraps.

Common visual elements:
- patched tartans mixed with frontier cloth and stolen heraldic remnants
- saint medals hammered from broken chain links
- instrument straps covered in prayer knots, beer stains, and trophies
- work kilts, riding skirts, layered coats, boots rebuilt too many times
- striped scarves in burgundy, teal, smoke-black, brass, and bottle-green
- faces painted before battle not for intimidation but for recognition
- armor plates engraved with joke inscriptions and memorial lines
- visible repairs treated as honor marks rather than flaws

When they ride into view, they should look like a carnival that got religion in exactly the wrong way.

### Sensory Read on Arrival

The Saints should be recognizable before they are visible.

First comes vibration through boards, bridge timbers, or packed road.

Then smell: hot iron, spilled beer, lamp smoke, damp wool, leather, pine pitch, ozone, and somebody definitely cooking onions somewhere they should not.

Then the music arrives in layers: a pipe line like a dare, drumbeat like boots on hollow doors, shouted chorus, fiddle thread, bell clang, crowd answer.

Then the wagon.

Lanterns swinging. Sparks coughing from the boar skull vents. Massive wheels rolling mud and gravel into the verge. Figures on the war porch already playing as if they were late to their own miracle.

### Social Role in the World

Different communities interpret the Saints differently.

To farmers, they are proof the wider world still remembers the road.

To children, they are myth made splintery and loud.

To refugees, they are sometimes the first safe fire in days.

To priests, they are either beloved embarrassments or borderline heretics.

To magistrates, they are permitting problems given religious protection by public popularity.

To fascists, supremacists, purity cults, and anyone trying to turn fear into doctrine, they are catastrophic. The Saints make it very difficult to keep people solemn in the precise way authoritarian movements require.

To musicians, they are either legends or absolute bastards depending on whether the Saints stole their chorus last season.

### Why the Shepherd Would Hate Them

This is worth making explicit.

The Shepherd’s Flock uses music as transmission: coordinated emotional architecture, grievance hardened into identity, chorus as permission structure for dehumanization, ritual synchronization as a precursor to transformation and consumption.

The Saints ruin that machinery.

They do not counter with purer doctrine. They counter with too much personhood. Their songs call names. Their choruses improvise. Their performances invite interruption, local verses, jokes, grief, profanity, and refusal. They make passive audiences active. They are hard to enthrall because they are always already participating. They are harder to convert because they offer belonging without requiring narrowing.

A clever cultist would study them. A desperate cultist would try to infiltrate them. A frightened cultist would simply want them dead.

### Signature Practices and Rituals

#### The Last Verse
When somebody dies under Saint protection or in Saint company, the final chorus of the next performance is altered to include their name. If the dead person was unknown, the verse says so plainly. Unknowns are not erased; they are named as not-yet-known.

#### The Nailing-In
Every new full member drives a single nail into the wagon somewhere meaningful. The placement is remembered. Older Saints claim you can hear old members in the creak patterns during storms.

#### The Open Cask
A settlement hosting the Saints is invited to contribute one cup from every household to a shared cask: ale, broth, tea, cider, diluted wine, milk, or water. The mixture is boiled, spiced, and served back. It is disgusting about one year in three. It is beloved every year.

#### The Mock Sermon
When the Saints suspect a local power figure deserves public correction, Rowan or another lead will begin what sounds like a prayer and slowly transform it into an indictment while the crowd catches up. This has caused riots, reform, marriage proposals, and at least one assassination attempt.

#### The Mosh of Witness
In extremis, the Saints will form a tightening rhythmic ring around the panicking, the charmed, or the near-broken, using percussion and bodily pressure to keep someone present until they can be spoken back into themselves. It is half dance, half triage.

### Mechanical or Narrative Talents You Can Lean On

Without fully statting them, the Saints can plausibly contribute in the following ways:

- break charm effects, crowd panic, or ritual synchronization through counter-performance
- transport civilians through active danger
- fortify a crossroads or inn in minutes using wagon panels and stage braces
- turn a rest stop into a rumor hub
- create cover for covert action through noise, celebration, or staged public scandal
- serve as living intelligence network across multiple Reaches
- smuggle people, instruments, texts, or dangerous relics hidden inside cask compartments
- escalate a quiet town into a politically inconvenient celebration
- draw enemies away by being too obvious to ignore

### Costs, Complications, and Failure Modes

They should not be easy allies.

The Saints bring problems.

They are loud when stealth would be easier. They humiliate officials who might otherwise have been useful. They attract drifters, romantics, thieves, local musicians with opinions, and people whose grief becomes visible after the third song. They can turn low simmer tensions into open civic energy faster than anyone planned for. They have internal fractures around faith, discipline, risk, and how far to push public provocation.

They also risk mythologizing themselves. Any faction built around joy and resistance eventually risks becoming addicted to entrance, legend, and the righteousness of being seen as liberators. That tension is useful. It makes them feel human rather than mascot-like.

### Adventure and Module Hooks

**1. The Counter-Hymn Fragment**  
A broken verse surfaces that only the Saints know how to finish. If completed wrongly it empowers an enemy ritual. If completed correctly it can break one.

**2. The Missing Last Cask**  
The Longhouse’s emergency saint-cask has been stolen. Everyone assumes it contains miraculous drink. In truth it contains names, vows, maps, and one terrible relic the Saints swore never to lose.

**3. False Saints on the Road**  
A copycat gang is using Saint colors and songs to loot settlements and inflame tensions. The real Saints need the lie killed before they lose their social immunity.

**4. The Wheel That Started Dreaming**  
One of the windmill-sized wheels has begun speaking in groans, dream-lines, and fragments of old names. Something in its salvaged wood or root core is waking.

**5. The Defector’s Chorus**  
A whole cluster of ex-cult singers want in, but one of them is still carrying the cadence of enthrallment. Are they victims, infiltrators, or both?

**6. The Longhouse Wants a Bell**  
Brother Ash insists a particular cracked bell in a flooded shrine is necessary to complete a protective instrument array. Retrieving it means dealing with whatever has been singing beneath the water.

**7. Riot at a Respectable Festival**  
The Saints are invited to perform at a civic event specifically because officials think they can be turned into safe pageantry. This goes badly.

**8. The Saint Who Stayed**  
A former member settled somewhere years ago, became respectable, and now begs the wagon not to expose who they used to be. Unfortunately the past is physically returning.

**9. The Wagon Versus the Impossible Bridge**  
To reach a besieged community, the Longhouse must cross a structure no sane engineer would trust. The Saints’ answer is to play harder.

**10. The Child Who Already Knows the Chorus**  
A local child somehow knows an old Saint song no one outside the fellowship should know. That means someone lost, hidden, dead, or trapped is still out there.

### Table Use: How to Make Them Feel Right

If you deploy the Saints, do not play them as polished fantasy rockstars. Play them as overcommitted road family. They interrupt one another. They carry gear while arguing theology. They swear during blessings. They repair things mid-conversation. Their holiness is inseparable from labor, appetite, noise, and public affection.

Do not make them simply cool. Make them specific.

A Saint should hand a refugee stew with one hand and tune a warpipe with the other. A Saint should be able to make a child laugh and a magistrate sweat in the same minute. The Longhouse should feel too large for the road and yet exactly the shape of the gap in the world when it arrives.

Most importantly: they are not there to replace the party. They are there to alter the emotional and social physics of a scene. They widen possibility. They make fear louder and therefore more survivable. They turn roads into stages, stages into barricades, and barricades into promises.

### Canon-Safe Summary

The Raucous Saints fit Elderwyld because the setting already contains all the pressures that would create them: mixed peoples who built a new world after escaping authoritarian order, civic identity tied to brewing and festival culture, dangerous frontier distances measured in Reaches, the need for moving mutual aid, and an existing war over song, public feeling, and ritual control.

They do not need a fixed headquarters. They do not need a single patron. They do not need to be placed in one exact continuity slot to work. They are a mobile answer to recurring conditions in the world.

When Elderwyld becomes too quiet in the wrong way, too frightened, too obedient, too isolated, too humorless, or too close to letting the bad song win, the road eventually answers with bagpipes.

And then the wheels arrive.


### Quick Tables for Use at the Table

#### d8 First Impressions of the Saints
1. The sound arrives before they do, and by the time the wagon appears everyone nearby is already emotionally involved.
2. They look like a battle line and a tavern crowd got married in a forge.
3. Someone is laughing, someone is repairing a wheel, someone is blessing a child, and all three actions seem equally serious.
4. The giant wheels make the road feel too small for the world.
5. The boar-skull prow is horrifying until you notice the painted flowers around one tusk and the ribbons tied to the railings.
6. Every visible part of the wagon has been repaired at least twice and loved at least once.
7. Their arrival makes respectable people stand straighter and exhausted people start crying.
8. It feels less like visitors have arrived and more like the road itself has decided to intervene.

#### d8 Rumors People Tell About Them
1. They once drove the Longhouse through a haunted toll gate and the gate still opens for musicians only.
2. Their best keg can cure cowardice, heartbreak, and possession, but only if you drink it while singing.
3. One of their wheels is made from the remains of an impossible tree that still hates axes.
4. They know the true rebel songs from the first escape and refuse to sing them for anyone taking attendance.
5. A Saint can tell when a crowd is about to become a mob by the taste of the air.
6. They keep a list of every child rescued on the road and add a nail for each one.
7. If you hear their pipes during a storm, count your family immediately; the wagon never arrives for no reason.
8. The Longhouse has died three times and keeps rolling out of spite.

#### d6 Reasons the Saints Might Refuse a Job
1. The requester wants protection but refuses to feed refugees, non-humans, or other “undesirables.”
2. The job is clean on paper and morally rotten in practice.
3. The requester wants the Saints as pageantry, not people.
4. The Saints suspect they are being used to start a riot rather than protect a vulnerable crowd.
5. The requested target is another road family, caravan, or moving sanctuary.
6. The price is good, but the song they hear underneath it is wrong.

### Final Note

If you want the Saints to land hard, let them be too large for the scene at first. Then let the scene reveal why something that loud had to exist.
