## Mossford

Map Key & Accompanying Gazetteer

A practical GM reference for the river town of Mossford — its streets, civic heart, working districts, and the moss-fed economy that keeps the whole place alive. This document is written to accompany the current town map and give each labeled location enough texture to play at the table.

Figure 1. Current working town map of Mossford.

## Quick Key

## Gazetteer

## 1. Watch Tower

The Watch Tower stands on Old Settlement Hill, a stubborn relic from the years when this country still thought every horizon might be hostile. The structure is squat at the base and narrow at the crown, built more for endurance than elegance. From below it looks almost austere, but once a lantern is lit in the top chamber the whole hill seems to wake around it.

On clear days the tower serves as a weather eye. At dawn, watchers study cloud lines and wind direction; at dusk, they mark river color, reservoir height, and any shimmer moving through the foothill mist. When danger comes, the tower becomes a civic drum. Its bells are not musical. They are blunt instruments of urgency — flood, fire, breach, strangers, or forest movement — and every local knows the difference between one peal and three quick strikes.

At the table, the tower is the best place to read the region. It gives a commanding view of the river, the dam, the paddies, and the road south. It is also where a worried mayor, an exhausted ranger, or a sleepless scout would naturally go when they need perspective more than company.

## 2. Temple of the Nameless Stone

This temple is smaller and plainer than the great religious architecture of Mirathorn, but that is precisely the point. Mossford does not advertise holiness with grandeur. Its shrine to the Nameless Goddess is built of old stone and dark moss-timber, with a threshold worn smooth by muddy boots, wet skirts, and work-callused hands. It smells faintly of lamp oil, river clay, and dried reed incense.

Locals come here for quiet things: blessing a harvest basin before flooding it, naming a child, speaking over the dead, or asking that a dangerous season pass with minimal cruelty. The altar is modest. The offerings are practical. Bundled moss ribbon, polished river stones, a cup of dark beer, a handful of seed. People kneel here not because they are theatrical, but because they are tired and need somewhere to put their fear for an hour.

In play, this is the town’s moral hearth. It is where confessions happen in low voices, where the old remember the first hard years, and where a character can feel the shape of Mossford’s values: endurance, service, and the belief that survival itself is sacred.

## 3. Stoneweir Dam

The Stoneweir Dam is the oldest piece of serious infrastructure in the region and, depending on who you ask, the true founder of Mossford. The town exists because this wall exists. It holds the river back just enough to create the reservoir that feeds the paddies, but not so much that it strangles the water completely. Every sluice, spillway, and gate has a history attached to it, usually in the form of somebody’s grandfather nearly dying while fixing it.

The dam is a place of spray, thunder, and constant work. Mosswood braces have been bolted into old stone where floods cracked the original face. Ironmoss panels seal repairs so well that the older locals now judge craftsmanship by whether the patched section looks older than the wall beside it. In heavy weather, crews move along the catwalks with hooked poles, mallets, and shouted measurements, adjusting flow before the reservoir can overtop.

For table use, the dam is an excellent crisis site. It is loud, dangerous, cinematic, and socially important. Sabotage here is catastrophic. A defense mounted here feels desperate and physical. A conversation held here must be shouted over the rush, which often means only the truth survives the noise.

## 4. Copper Moss Brewery & Waterwheel Mill

If Town Hall is where Mossford speaks, the brewery and mill are where Mossford laughs, argues, and lies. The waterwheel turns with a deep wet clatter all day, and the attached millhouse and brewhouse never quite stop moving. Grain is husked here. Moss fibers are pulped here. Ale is brewed here. News is absolutely distorted here.

The brewery’s signature smell is sweet mash cut with tannic moss and warm copper. The walls sweat in summer. The floors are forever damp. Workers know how to carry on full conversations without pausing for the machinery, which means strangers often struggle for a few minutes before they realize everyone here is effectively shouting. By evening, the place softens. Labor turns to drink, and all the practical people who swore they were going straight home begin drifting toward one more cup.

In play, this location is useful because it is both industrial and social. Characters can find workers, gossip, transport hands, and shift captains here. A rumor heard in the brewery feels different from one heard in a shrine: less filtered, more immediate, and usually attached to somebody’s cousin who swears they saw it themselves.

## 5. Market Square

Market Square is not huge, but it is busy in the particular way of a town whose economy is material, local, and real. Mossford does not posture like a grand trade city. It bargains. The square is ringed by open-front shops, counting sheds, cooper stalls, rope sellers, ferrymen, and peddlers with drying racks strapped to handcarts. Most goods have mud on them, which locals take as proof that something useful happened before the sale.

This is where the entire town crosses itself. Farmers come in from the south. Millworkers cut through on the way home. Children are sent on errands they immediately complicate. Visiting traders pretend to be unimpressed by moss-lumber until they ask the price of a beam. Notices are read aloud here. Disputes are aired here. When the mayor needs to say something difficult, she says it where people can mutter back.

At the table, Market Square is the natural place for rumors, recruitment, unrest, or a public promise the party may later regret making. It should feel like the compression point of the town — the place where private problems acquire witnesses.

## 6. Town Hall

Town Hall rises above the square with enough dignity to look official without seeming distant. It is not a palace. It is a civic tool. Mossford built it in the style the town admires most: thick walls, clear lines, and no decorative choice that cannot survive weather, soot, and decades of argument. The upper windows look out over the market and north bank, while the lower rooms are given over to ledgers, maps, and the constant burden of public requests.

Inside, the place is all order and paper. Reservoir records, land claims, mold schedules, work rosters, tax notes, flood marks, bridge inspections. The council chamber is plain but serious; every scratch on the table looks earned. The mayor’s office feels used rather than staged, with rolled maps, wet boots by the wall, and correspondence that always seems to come in faster than it can be answered.

For play, Town Hall is where players meet civic authority that feels local rather than abstract. It is the right place for briefings, awkward negotiations, emergency planning, and evidence laid out on a table while everyone realizes the problem is worse than they hoped.

## 7. Moldyards

The Moldyards are the least romantic part of Mossford and one of the most important. This district is where living harvest becomes construction material. Fresh-cut moss slabs arrive slick, green-black, and soft with water weight. They leave as hard, resilient boards, ribs, shutters, braces, and wall-panels that can rival seasoned timber and, in some cases, shame it.

The yards are crowded with stone forms, pressure frames, steaming vats, curing sheds, hanging racks, and hammering stations. The work is exacting. Too much heat and a piece warps. Too little pressure and it cures spongy. Every yardmaster has a theory about why their method is superior, and every apprentice spends at least a year burning fingertips and pretending not to be shouted at. There is a fierce craft pride here, but it expresses itself through quality rather than poetry.

At the table, the Moldyards give you labor tension, industrial hazards, and skilled NPCs with strong opinions. They are ideal for sabotage, guild politics, specialized crafting, or any scene where the party needs to understand that Mossford is not merely surrounded by its economy — it is built from it.

## 8. Mossford Inn

The inn sits where a traveler wants it: close enough to the main crossings to be useful, far enough from the loudest industry to allow sleep. It is broad rather than tall, with a deep common room, a kitchen that knows the value of hot broth, and stables built for wet gear and tired animals rather than elegance. The sign outside has been repainted often enough that the lettering is slightly different each generation.

The common room serves as a neutral ground between locals and passers-through. Carters, dam crews, field hands, ferrymen, and visiting merchants all end up here by nightfall, and the room develops a rhythm of clatter, stew steam, and practical talk. Outsiders often mistake the place for quiet because Mossford is quieter than Mirathorn. That impression usually lasts right up until three different tables start arguing about river levels at once.

In play, the inn is your resting place, briefing room, rumor exchange, and easiest soft-entry into town life. If the map is the skeleton of Mossford, the inn is one of the places where the blood actually moves.

## 9. Dense Worker Housing

This district is the living body of the town: close-built houses, attached sheds, narrow cut-throughs, raised stoops, rooflines almost touching, laundry strung over alleys, and children learning to run on planks over drainage ditches before they can write their names. A stranger might call it crowded. A local would call it efficient.

These homes are practical, multi-use, and visibly inhabited. Lower floors often hold tools, curing shelves, or work benches; upper rooms hold beds, family shrines, and the accumulated evidence of people refusing to live abstractly. Cooking smells drift through the lanes. So does gossip. Nothing here is sealed off for long. If a fight breaks out, six windows open. If a child goes missing, twenty people notice.

At the table, this district is where the town feels personal. Chases are good here. Witnesses are good here. So are scenes of sudden solidarity — neighbors hauling sandbags, sharing kettles, or standing in a lane with crossed arms when something dangerous enters their part of town.

## 10. Stone Bridge

The Stone Bridge is the hinge between town and field. Built wide enough for carts, beasts, and two stubborn arguments to pass each other, it is less elegant than dependable. Mossford depends on the south-bank paddies, and the south-bank paddies depend on this bridge. Every basket of food, every slab of drying moss, every work crew, and every evacuation plan understands that fact.

The bridge has its own life by time of day. Morning: farm traffic northbound. Midday: mixed trade. Evening: workers going home with mud on their hems and damp bundles over their shoulders. In wet weather the stones darken almost black, and the river below sounds close enough to make strangers nervous. In an emergency, people glance to the bridge before they glance to each other.

For play, this is a powerful threshold location. It is where a town can split itself between safety and necessity, where a defense line looks plausible, and where the party can feel the physical truth of logistics: if this crossing fails, Mossford changes instantly.

## 11. Drying Barns

South of the bridge, among the ordered basins and raised paths, the Drying Barns sit low and long against the land. Their interiors are warmer than the air outside, full of layered racks, suspended slabs, pulley rigs, and the smell of wet plant matter slowly turning into something hard and permanent. To anyone who understands Mossford, these barns feel almost miraculous: here is the moment when a field becomes a wall, a gate, a roof beam.

Workers in the barns move with practiced patience. Fresh material must be set, turned, spaced, checked, and sometimes trimmed by hand. The work is not glamorous, but it is intimate with the town’s core technology. Apprentices learn quickly that what looks like simple drying is actually controlled transformation, and mistakes can ruin a week’s harvest.

In play, the barns are excellent for concealment, ambush, supply scenes, or eerie quiet before trouble. They also communicate something essential about Mossford: even its architecture spends time as agriculture first.

## 12. Southern Road

The Southern Road is the line that ties Mossford to Mirathorn and, through Mirathorn, to the broader settled shape of the Elderwyld. It runs out through the farms in a clear, practical cut, wide enough for wagons and steady enough that locals judge the season by what the road is carrying. Dry months mean traffic and goods. Wet months mean repairs and careful wheels. Crisis means dust or mud and too many people moving too quickly.

Unlike the market or the bridge, the road carries a certain emotional charge. It is arrival and departure both. Young people leave by it. Goods leave by it. News comes by it. Trouble also comes by it, and the town knows the difference between a merchant string and a frightened caravan almost at a glance.

At the table, the Southern Road is the easiest way to stage changing pressure on Mossford. Reinforcements appear here. Refugees appear here. Dead silence on the road can be just as ominous as heavy traffic. It is the map’s cleanest vector between local life and the wider campaign.

**GM Use Note: **Mossford should feel materially specific. Almost every conversation can be tied back to water, labor, moss, timing, or the fear of losing what the town has built. That practical texture is what makes danger here land harder.
